# 3. WHAT SNIFFIES IS DOING WITH ITS PRICING

Sniffies is using the weekly subscription as an extremely high anchor.

$11.99/week annualized to a monthly equivalent is approximately:

**$51.96/month**

That explains the checkout labels:

| Sniffies Plan | Monthly Equivalent | Discount Badge |
|---|---:|---:|
| Weekly continuously | ~$51.96 | baseline |
| 1 Month | $19.99 | ~62% |
| 3 Months | $12.99 | ~75% |
| 6 Months | $9.99 | ~81% |

This is clever pricing psychology.

It makes $9.99/month for six months look extremely inexpensive despite requiring a **$59.94 commitment**.

### Piroka should learn from this—but not copy it exactly.

For Piroka, savings should primarily be presented against the normal **monthly plan**, because that is easier for customers to understand.

For example:

> **3 Months — Save 17%**  
> **6 Months — Save 33%**

That is cleaner and more transparent than advertising an 80%+ discount derived from continuously buying weekly access.

# 30. PRICE INCREASE GUARDRAILS

Run the higher price against a controlled holdout.

Do not permanently roll it out if:

| KPI | Failure threshold |
|---|---:|
| Revenue/paywall viewer | ↓ >10% |
| Checkout conversion | ↓ >20% relative |
| Refund rate | >3% |
| Paid D30 retention | ↓ >2 points |
| Cancellations | ↑ >20% relative |

The KPI is not:

> “Did conversion decrease?”

A higher price normally lowers conversion somewhat.

The KPI is:

> **Did net value/revenue improve without damaging retention or network health?**
